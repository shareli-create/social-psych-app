Social functions of emotion: Introduction to Section
Brian Parkinson, University of Oxford, UK

The emotions that a person expresses or enacts can affect other people’s cognitions, emotions and behaviours in various ways.  Some theorists argue that the function of some socially oriented emotions is precisely to exert these interpersonal effects.  Evolutionary psychologists suggest that social functions are built-in to some biologically basic emotions.  Social constructionists and other cultural relativists argue that emotions may also develop over the course of socialization to meet more localized societal challenges.  This chapter sets out a range of views on emotion’s social functionality.  In particular, it draws a distinction between functions that depend on the way that the expressed emotion is interpreted by perceivers and those served by lower-level processes of interpersonal adjustment and cuing.  The aim is to set an agenda for researching emotion’s social effects and making sense of cross-cultural similarities and differences.


Emotions in social context
Think of the last time you felt emotional.  Maybe someone said something offensive or congratulated you.  Perhaps you were worrying about an upcoming meeting or interview.  In either case, chances are that someone else was involved in some way or other (Shaver, Wu, & Schwartz, 1992).  Emotions don’t only happen in social situations, but even privately experienced emotions often carry social implications.
In the next chapters, you will read about how people perceive other people’s emotions, how emotions operate in interpersonal, group and organizational settings, and how cultural factors shape the way that emotional episodes unfold in all these situations.  You will learn how the wider social world can shape the initiation, maintenance, expression, regulation and perception of emotional processes.  But what does all this tell us about emotions themselves?  Does investigating emotions in their social context also clarify how they work more generally?
This chapter addresses these theoretical issues by considering alternative views of the ways in which emotions can serve social functions.  The purpose is to set an agenda for interpreting the diverse findings presented in later chapters.  I shall argue that the principles of natural selection cannot fully explain cultural differences, and that cultural differences run deeper than variations in emotion concepts or display rules.  Emotions are designed to align relations between people, objects and events in contexts that have culture-specific characteristics and meanings.  Socialization serves the evolutionary function of attuning individuals to the opportunities and demands of the surrounding social world and providing appropriate practices for addressing them (Parkinson, 2012).
The following sections pave the way for these conclusions.  First, I will discuss how evolutionary theories attempt to explain the social functions of emotion.  Second, I will review approaches that emphasize the role of culture in accounting for emotion’s functions.  Third, I will consider what kinds of evidence support the idea that emotion and emotion regulation can serve social functions.  Finally, I turn to the processes underlying emotional influence and suggest that people do not need to register the emotional meaning of another person’s expression to be affected by it.  Correspondingly, not all cultural differences in emotion’s social effects are directly mediated by variations in emotion-related norms, beliefs or values.
Evolutionary accounts
Why do humans have emotions?  A common answer is that they serve adaptive functions that enhance the prospects of survival and reproduction.  If some of our ancestors happened to be equipped with emotions that made it more likely that their genes were passed down the generations, then their emotions would have been inherited too.  As the species developed, those same emotions would become increasingly prevalent human characteristics.  In other words, emotions may have been subject to the forces of natural selection.
Directly adaptive functions
Many of the adaptive functions attributed to emotions are about surviving in the face of the practical challenges faced by proto-humans operating as hunter-gatherers in environments that are quite different from the ones people experience today.  Many theorists argue that the emotions were hard-wired into our brains way back in evolutionary history to serve adaptive functions that were relevant then but not necessarily now (e.g., Tooby & Cosmides, 1990).  For example, dealing with predators out in the wild savannah is not a common problem for contemporary office workers, service personnel or IT technicians.
Perhaps the most commonly discussed survival functions are fight and flight.  According to evolutionary accounts, anger is a preprogrammed strategy that prepares an individual for battle with an antagonist in appropriate circumstances and fear is a preprogrammed strategy that prepares an individual to run away.  If these emotions successfully served those functions in the early history of our species, then the brain modules that supposedly control them would have been selected in.  However, that doesn’t mean that getting angry or fearful when the old environmental triggers are present continues to aid survival nowadays.  Instead, our anger may provoke unnecessary antagonism that ends up hurting more than helping us and our fear may encourage us to seek escape in situations that we would otherwise have the capacity to deal with.
For emotions to work in this way, we need to assume that some heritable mechanism detects situations for which the emotion provides an adaptive response (i.e., some kind of innately specified appraisal) and that the emotion itself is able to coordinate that response in an adaptive way (e.g., by putting the mind and body in some kind of integrated action readiness mode, Tooby & Cosmides, 1990).  For example, the threat appraisal associated with fear would need to be attuned to stimulus patterns that indicated the potential proximity of a predator and the fear action readiness mode would need to get the body and mind ready for escaping from the appraised threat, perhaps by channelling metabolic energy to appropriate muscles and activating an urge to get away.  Because a wide range of patterned cues (sounds, sights, smells) might indicate the possible proximity of a predator (which could approach from many possible angles) the threat detection mechanism cannot be responsive only to single, specifiable stimuli.  And because no single set of movements aids escape under a wide-enough range of circumstances, the response package that gets elicited needs to be more flexible than a simple reflex.  The question then is how much flexibility at the input and output ends of the process is required to make emotions serve their adaptive functions.  And increasing flexibility means decreasing the integrity and co-ordination of the supposedly preprogrammed emotion.
Many appraisal theorists argue that the functional point of having emotions is that they provide a middle way between fixed reflexive responses and fully reasoned deliberate action (e.g., Smith & Ellsworth, 1987).  Reflexes engage rapidly but risk running off in inappropriate circumstances without the potential for adjustment.  Reasoning allows a more considered response but may take too long when situational requirements are urgent.  Emotions provide an adaptive solution that mitigates both sets of issue by restricting the range of considerations at the front end and constraining the possibilities of response at the back end.  It is not as quick and dirty as a reflex or as slow and clean as a fully reasoned response.
An alternative to this three-level classification of possible responses would be to suggest that people adjust the amount of thinking they do before acting depending on the level of urgency.  This would mean that distinctions between reflexes, emotions, and reasoned decisions are less clearcut, making it possible that “emotions” can operate at different possible levels of speed and flexibility.
Social functions
How do social functions fit into this picture?  Early humans not only needed to deal with predators but also to get on with other members of their community in order to survive.  Passing on their genes to future generations also required them to navigate sexual relationships and to ensure that their offspring survived long enough to reproduce too.
Exactly how emotions need to be configured to meet these social challenges is harder to specify.  Evolutionary psychologists usually focus on how emotions make people more sensitive to certain social concerns thereby motivating them to take appropriate action.  For example, Shackelford, Buss, and Bennett (2002) proposed that jealousy is designed to prevent male sexual partners from wasting valuable resources on caring for offspring who might not be carrying their genes.  By contrast, they claim that female jealousy is more attuned to potential threats to partner’s contributions to caring for offspring.  Of course, cultural socialisation might also explain any differences between the factors that tend to trigger jealousy in men and women living in contemporary Anglo-American society.
Speculations about selective pressures that might explain emotional predilections in contemporary Anglo-American societies are hard to substantiate.  We cannot go back in time to the Pleistocene epoch or observe what happened over a long enough period to capture the gradual impact of natural selection on the human gene pool.  Fossil records reveal little about early human brains were configured.  All this leaves us with a range of possible origin stories that seem to broadly fit with our current views of human nature.
Most evolutionary accounts assume that the processes shaping human emotional activity were ingrained in fixed brain structures long before any of us were born.  Any adjustment to changing environmental circumstances would then require several successive generations before taking effect.  However, evidence about historical changes and cultural differences suggests that emotional response syndromes may also emerge and consolidate within a more restricted time-scale.  Natural selection is ill equipped to account for these more rapid developments.  Perhaps then cultural evolution can provide a more viable explanation.
Evolution vs. socialization
Unlike many other animals, human beings undergo a long period stretching through infancy and childhood before reaching maturity (neoteny).  One adaptive consequence is that they can gradually acquire the skills and practices of the surrounding culture over the course of socialisation.  This increases the human capacity for flexible adjustment to different historical circumstances.  Instead of having to rely only on hard-wired mechanisms that allow them to cope independently with persistent challenges to reproduction and survival, humans can exploit the collected wisdom and expertise developed in more specific cultural circumstances and tailored by the socialization agents with whom they have most contact.  Some of the useful practices that they acquire are emotional.  For example, children may learn when and how expressing frustration in different ways can result in them receiving concessions or disapproval from other people depending on the norms of their society (Cole, Bruschi, & Tamang, 2002).
The fact that young humans depend on other people to care for them means that they need to be attuned to what those other people do and to behave in ways that solicit the appropriate nurturing responses.  Any innate capacities for emotional engagement seem more likely to be oriented to these relationship-maintaining challenges than to more direct survival-related concerns.  After all, an innate fear response that prepared for flight would be of little use for a baby who has not yet learned to crawl.  In the unlikely event of being left stranded in the wild with no parent nearby, crying for help seems a far more adaptive strategy should a sabre-toothed tiger suddenly enter the scene.
Cultural functions
Being part of a wider group or society facilitates survival and reproduction.  Some human capacities may have developed to deal with the universal challenges associated with getting on with other people.  For example, people from all societies need to be able to affiliate with others under appropriate circumstances.  They also need to limit the extent to which they might be exploited by other people.  However, the specific risks and opportunities presented by the social world also differ depending on how society is arranged.  This means that many forms of emotional life may be designed to serve culturally specific functions.  Sometimes this involves similar emotions operating in different ways.  For example, people living in an honour culture where self-worth is strongly associated with public reputation are more likely to get angry about insults that threaten their social image than people living in a dignity culture where self-worth has a more personal basis (Maitner et al., 2017).  The same emotion (e.g., pride or its equivalent) responds to the same concern (e.g., self-worth) in both kinds of society, but this concern is activated under different culturally relevant circumstances.  However, cultural differences in emotion can also run deeper than this.  For example, emotions such as the Japanese experience of amae (e.g., Morsbach & Tyler, 1986) seem to be designed to address concerns that have no direct equivalent outside their local societal context.  Although these emotions may serve indirect evolutionary functions by facilitating acceptance in the wider community, their more direct purpose seems to be about regulating relationships on a tighter scale.
A range of different social functions have been attributed to emotions, some of which may be more salient or important in some societies than in others.  Fischer and Manstead (2016) proposed a general distinction between affiliative functions and social distancing functions.  Similarly, Kitayama, Mesquita and Karasawa (2006) distinguished socially engaging emotions (e.g., affection) from socially disengaging emotions (e.g., anger) and argued that individualistic societies are relatively more likely to encourage the latter whereas collectivistic societies are relatively more likely to encourage the latter.
Understanding the functions of specific emotions requires more fine-grained distinctions.  Both social approach (engagement, affiliation) and social withdrawal (disengagement, distancing) can be broken down into subcategories for this purpose.  For example, social withdrawal may involve avoidance in the case of shame or active escape in the case of fear.  Distinctions based on the intended form of social influence are also potentially useful.  For example, appeasement emotions such as embarrassment or guilt serve to mitigate other people’s negative responses to your conduct, whereas supplication emotions such as disappointment or sadness serve as requests for care or comfort from other people (e.g., van Kleef et al. 2006).  As with approach and withdrawal, these lower-level functions may be relevant to all kinds of human societies.  However, their relative prominence and the behavioural strategies used to address them are likely to vary.  Indeed, it seems possible that culturally specific variants of these functions emerge in cultures that value honour, status or face-saving.
Emotions, norms and functions don’t necessarily map onto each other one-to-one.  Averill’s (1980) social constructionist theory argues that emotions serve the function of navigating situations where two different cultural norms conflict with one another.  For example, Western anger is seen as a short-term social role designed to serve a retaliatory purpose when someone else has infringed your rights, thereby satisfying retributive norms (Averill, 1982).  Although deliberate aggressive responses would fall foul of pacifistic norms, the apparent uncontrollability of the angry response makes it more forgiveable.  Something comes over the angry individual rather than them choosing willfully to threaten their antagonist.  Similarly, Averill (1985; Averill & Boothroyd, 1977) presents romantic love as a strategy for defending the importance of the individual under historical circumstances where only members of the elite were valued for their personal accomplishments.  Each party to a romantic relationship gets a chance to be seen as special by someone else in at least one domain of their life.
It is not clear whether norm conflict is a necessary precondition for the emergence of every kind of culturally functional emotion.  However, an advantage of Averill’s theory is its ability to address the puzzle of how emotions can achieve apparently intended effects without the emotional actor explicitly deciding to get emotional.  In a sense, the emotional person disowns the evident purpose of what they are trying to do (Griffiths, 1997).  Another possible explanation for the passive purposiveness of socially functional emotions is that environmental cues and other people’s responses to emotional enactment scaffold the actor’s performance in a way that minimizes the need for any articulated strategy (see Mesquita & Parkinson, 2024; Parkinson, 2019).
Functional emotion regulation
Emotions do not always operate fully outside the individual’s control.  Research into emotion regulation shows how people often try to make themselves either more or less emotional than they would otherwise have been (and sometimes succeed).  Two common motives for emotion regulation are to increase pleasure and to reduce displeasure.  However, people also exert control over their emotions to gain other kinds of benefit or reduce other kinds of cost, even when it does not make them feel better in the short term (Tamir, 2009). If emotions help an individual to adjust to local norms or influence other people in ways that further their goals, then there are good reasons for wanting to deploy them for personal advantage.  Even if the desired emotion feels bad, its anticipated beneficial outcomes can compensate for the temporary hedonic downsides.
People can work up their emotions in a variety of ways (e.g., Gross, 1998).  For example, you may try to focus your attention on the most irritating aspects of what is happening or try to see the other person as more blameworthy to make yourself angry.  Alternatively, you may simply simulate the facial and verbal expressions of the intended emotion.  If other people respond to your simulated presentation as if it were genuine, this may encourage your expression to become even more authentic.  Indeed, if you make someone else think you are angry, they may well get angry back and this may fuel further anger on your part.
There is some evidence that upregulating emotions using these methods can influence other people’s behaviour in personally advantageous ways.  For example, Sinaceur and Tiedens (2006) found that participants who were encouraged to express more anger in a role-played negotiation achieved better outcomes when the other negotiator had relatively lower power than they did.
Even when emotion regulation is not motivated by a desire to influence other people, it may serve cultural functions by increasing social integration.  For example, if US society values activated positive affective states such as exuberance more than deactivated positive affective states such as calm and serenity (Tsai, 2007), then North Americans who work up their excitement and enthusiasm are more likely to fit in.  Selective deployment of socially engaging emotions in collectivistic societies potentially brings similar benefits (e.g., Kitayama et al., 2006).
Demonstrating functionality
Most of the evidence supporting the social functionality of emotions in fact demonstrates interpersonal effects rather than functions.  For example, the finding that anger can help negotiators achieve better deals when the other bargainer has relatively less power (e.g., van Kleef et al, 2004) seems to suggest that this emotion’s purpose is to maximise rewards in that kind of interpersonal situation.  However, the beneficial outcomes might simply be fortuitous side effects of a different agenda.  One way of reducing this interpretational ambiguity is to show that the emotion occurs more often or is expressed to a greater extent under circumstances when the relevant functional concerns are stronger or more salient.  For example, husbands in distressed marriages become less aggressive when their wives exhibit depressed behaviour suggesting that the appeasing function of depression works against the assertive function of anger (Biglan et al., 1985), at least in Anglo American contexts.
Studies that track the dependency of emotion on changes in functionally relevant variables over time provide even stronger evidence.  For example, if an emotion is initiated at the moment that a functional concern arises then terminates as soon as the concern is dealt with, then we can be reasonably confident that it was oriented to addressing that concern.  Leary, Landel and Patten (1996) present findings about the social functionality of embarrassment that go some way towards meeting these conditions.  In their study, participants were either instructed to record their own rendition of a schmaltzy song or to listen to a rendition of the same song by a professional singer.  In the singing condition, participants were obviously concerned about how smooth their performance seemed to the main potential audience, namely the experimenter.  Some participants got a chance to communicate their embarrassment to the experimenter by completing a questionnaire about their feelings following the singing task.  Further, a subset of these participants saw the experimenter pick up their completed questionnaire, read it and nod in acknowledgement of having registered the relevant information.  These participants subsequently reported less embarrassment than those who did not see the experimenter look at their responses and than those who had not completed any prior questionnaire (who were the most embarrassed of all).  In other words, when the appeasement signal conveyed by embarrassment reached its intended target, the emotion tended to die away.  The obvious implication is that embarrassment serves an appeasement function.
One ambiguity of Leary and colleagues’ findings concerns whether the motivated presentation of embarrassment to the experimenter reflected authentic feelings or simply regulated expressions.  In practice, it is often hard to determine whether functional effects work by encouraging participants to regulate their emotions for social reasons or activate functional emotions more directly.  However, there are also corresponding conceptual issues about whether emotion and emotion regulation are wholly distinct phenomena in the first place (e.g. Kappas, 2011).
As well as aligning and realigning interpersonal relationships, emotions can also serve social functions that relate to intragroup and intergroup processes.  For example, sharing similar emotional orientations to what is happening can enhance the sense of shared group identity (e.g., Neville & Reicher, 2015) just as it increases social cohesion within societies more broadly.  Correspondingly, adopting emotional orientations that are distinctive from those of outgroups (or even antagonistic towards those outgroups) can bolster ingroup identification and collective self-esteem (e.g., Kessler & Hollbach, 2005).
Processes of emotional influence
So far, this chapter has focused on the question of why emotions might have social effects and functions rather than on the processes whereby those effects and functions are achieved.  In this final section, I consider possible mediators and mechanisms underlying the social influence that emotions can exert.
Meaning-mediated effects
When trying to explain how one person’s emotion influences another person, most theorists focus on the meaning that it conveys.  For example, anger may solicit concessions from lower-status negotiators because it provides information about what level of offer the angry person is prepared to consider (van Kleef et al., 2004).  In other words, the target of anger notices that the other person is angry and uses their knowledge about the implications of that emotion when deciding how to respond.  Hareli and Hess (2010) call this a reverse-engineering process because targets work backwards from the perceived emotion to make inferences about what must have caused it.  These inferences then mediate the interpersonal effects of the emotion.  More generally, this kind of social influence falls under the broader heading of social appraisal (Manstead & Fischer, 2001), because individuals are using information provided by another person’s emotional state to shape their appraisal of what is happening to them.  Their social appraisals may influence their own experienced emotions as well as their inferences about the meaning of current events.
Most social appraisal research involves presenting participants with information that indicates the specific emotion that another person is experiencing (often in the form of a verbal or written statement, e.g., Hareli & Hess, 2010, or a prototypical facial expression intended to represent a basic emotion, e.g., Hess & Hareli, 2018).  Under these circumstances, interpersonal effects are mediated by an intact categorical emotional meaning (e.g., “that person is angry/afraid/happy”).  However, it is also possible that separable components of the influencer’s emotional communication carry their own significance.  For example, Scherer and colleagues (2018) present evidence that perceivers pick up information about the novelty of what is happening from faces showing widened eyes and raised eyebrows in the absence of the other subcomponents of the emotional expressions that include this feature (e.g., fearful and surprised faces).  This raises the possibility that apparent effects of more articulated emotional meanings sometimes depend on lower-level inferences.  For example, Sorce and colleagues’ (1985) famous demonstration of social referencing seems to show that toddlers interpret their mother’s “fear” expression as indicating that crossing a visual cliff is dangerous.  However, we cannot be sure that participants in this study made inferences based on the overall patterned facial movements rather than the directed eye movements and raised eyebrows that are part of a “gasping” fear face.  Perhaps the toddlers were simply alerted to potentially significant features of the environment by their mother’s evidently intense visual attention.
Some of the meanings conveyed by emotional words and faces vary across cultures.  This applies not only to society-specific emotions such as Japanese amae but also to emotions that are more familiar in Anglo-American contexts (see Gendron et al, 2018, regarding facial expressions and Jackson et al., 2019, regarding emotion words).  For example, cultural norms about the appropriateness of different emotions can moderate interpretations of their expression.  In a society that discourages the expression of anger (e.g., Briggs, 1970), signs of that emotion not only indicate the usual appraisals of other-blame but also a willingness to flout conventions.  Other people’s negative responses to culturally inappropriate emotions may go some way toward explaining why individuals who express them tend to end up with lower general well-being (de Leersnyder, Kim, & Mesquita, 2015).
Non-inferential effects
Emotional influence does not only or always depend on registering the meaning of another person’s emotion.  Indeed, more direct processes might contribute to some of the lower-level informational effects discussed in the previous section.  For example, toddlers in Sorce and colleagues’ (1985) social referencing study may well have noticed that their mother’s fearful eyes were shifting urgently to the visual cliff but probably also adjusted their own gaze patterns as part of the mutual coordination of joint attention.  Directed gaze can serve as a cue that reorients another person’s gaze towards an object (e.g., Driver et al., 1999) as well as a source of information that that object is worth looking at.
Another non-inferential process that may contribute to emotional influence is automatic mimicry.  Some researchers believe that humans have an innate tendency to imitate other people’s movements (e.g., Meltzoff & Moore, 1977) including emotion-related facial movements (e.g., Field et al., 1982).  According to Hatfield, Cacioppo, and Rapson’s (1988) theory of primitive emotion contagion, interoceptive feedback from mimicked expressions then generates a corresponding emotion in the mimicker.
However, there is controversy about how emotional mimicry operates (e.g., Hess & Fischer, 2013).  Although some studies suggest that the process can operate implicitly outside participants’ awareness, it still seems to be sensitive to the meaning of the mimicked expressions rather than their structural features alone.  For example, Tamietto and colleagues (2009) showed that patients suffering from blindsight respond to visually presented postural expressions of happiness with facial movements relating to smiling suggesting that they had already detected the emotion associated with the stimulus and not just the configural features of the displayed posture.  This effect was observed even when the postural stimuli were presented in areas of the patients’ visual fields where they had no reportable consciousness of what they were seeing.  The findings therefore suggest that mimicry can be both automatic and meaning-dependent.
The fact that emotional expressions have dynamic temporal contours as well as configural properties raises the possibility of another form of meaning-independent emotional influence based on entrainment.  The idea here is that the rhythm of one person’s emotional behaviour tends to become synchronised with the rhythm of their interaction partner’s emotional behaviour as dialogue between the two people develops (e.g., Bernieri, Reznick & Rosenthal, 1988).  Relatively few studies have investigated the emotional consequences of interpersonal or intragroup entrainment (see Paez et al., 2015).  However, it seems theoretically possible that dynamic patterns of mutual engagement emerge as a function of reciprocal influence between two or more people’s unfolding streams of emotional behaviour (i.e., co-regulation, Butler & Randall, 2013).
Because non-inferential processes of emotional influence do not depend on the perceived meaning of emotion expressions, they seem less likely to be affected by cultural differences in emotion-related norms, beliefs and values.  However, cultural ideologies also shape institutional, political and informal practices that may have a more direct bearing on the modes of contact between individuals and groups.  For example, societies where women are encouraged to wear face coverings limit the range of emotional cues that can be transmitted (e.g., Fischer et al., 2012).  Although most studies investigating cultural differences focus on meaning-based effects, it is also worth considering other ways in which societies can shape emotional contact and interaction and their consequences for the social functionality of emotions.
Cumulative and combined effects
Outside the laboratory, emotional influence operates in a variety of different ways.  It rarely depends only on social appraisal or primitive emotion contagion but instead includes cuing, dynamic entrainment, social reinforcement and a range of other processes.  These processes usually do not operate in isolation from each other.  They intertwine over time to produce more complicated contextualised effects.  Influence also operates bidirectionally in interpersonal interactions and multidirectionally in group contexts.  This means that emotional consequences can emerge over time from reciprocal interactions as different processes consolidate into co-regulated patterns.  As a simple example, think about how members of a crowd of spectators respond together to the unfolding events of a football match, how their faces react when a goal is scored, simultaneously providing feedback to everyone else who is nearby.  Their appraisal of what is happening may be largely determined by prior attitudes and affiliations.  However, it also adjusts moment by moment to the convergent reactions of other fans.  Because everyone knows that they are in this together, their sense of emotional engagement does not require any detection of their own expressive reaction that then produces internal feedback.  Instead, the faces around them already tell them what they are feeling themselves (Parkinson, 2020).  The modes of contact between people in other kinds of cultural ritual can similarly shape the processes and outcomes of emotional influence.
Conclusions
Although natural selection has clearly played a role in shaping human emotions, processes of cultural socialisation and real-time adjustment to changing circumstances also underlie many of their social functions and effects.  Many emotions are attuned to other people’s responses to them and serve the purpose of aligning relations between two or more people and their respective orientations to objects and events in the shared environment (e.g., Parkinson, 2008).  Because these objects and events differ in different societies and have different meanings to members of those societies, emotional orientations to those objects and events are susceptible to cultural variations too.  In the chapters that follow, you will read more about how various emotion-related responses and processes are sensitive to culture-dependent considerations and concerns.  Together they provide a rich picture of the diversity of human emotional experiences and the social functions that they promote.
